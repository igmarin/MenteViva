# Statistics & claims rationale — 2026-09-22

One place that states what our numbers do and don't support, so no copy,
comment, or App Store line ever overclaims. Sources: `research-2026-09.md`
(evidence) and `jev-revisit.md` (judgments). Copy ceiling is policy, not a
suggestion: **"fun mental exercise / stay engaged."**

## 1. The three numbers we actually have

| Study | Design | Result | What it supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| CCT meta-analysis (51 RCTs) | computerized cognitive training, healthy 60+ | overall effect **g≈0.22**; gains concentrate on the *trained* tasks | "Playing a game exercises the skill it trains." Nothing more. |
| **ACTIVE**, 10-yr follow-up | N=2,802 RCT | speed-of-processing arm: dementia incidence 22.7% vs 28.8% control (**−29%**), dose-dependent; memory and reasoning arms: **no** effect | Strongest single result in the field — but specific to speed training with booster sessions, and singular. |
| **FINGER** | N=1,260, 2 yr, ages 60–77 | multidomain bundle (diet + exercise + cognitive training + vascular) improved NTB **z +0.022/yr** vs control (p=0.030) | Training works best *bundled with lifestyle* — supports our warm, habit-level framing, not games-alone claims. |

The mainstream anchor stays the **2014 Stanford/Max Planck consensus** (70
scientists): *no compelling evidence* brain games improve everyday cognition
or prevent decline. Population nuance: MCI is the best-supported target;
for established dementia the evidence shows no reliable benefit and for
early-stage dementia it is unclear. So our design serves early-stage players
(errorless, familiar) without ever *claiming* an effect on them.

## 2. How to read g≈0.22 honestly

- Small: a ~0.2 SD group-average shift. It is a *statistical* statement about
  groups, not a promise about any individual — and never about dad.
- Near transfer only: players get better at the game-like tasks, not provably
  at daily life. Far transfer is weak/unproven (ACTIVE's speed arm is the one
  qualified exception, ten years out, with boosters).
- A result being "the strongest in the field" and "small" are both true.
  Design implication: we compete on engagement and dignity, not on outcomes.

## 3. How to read Jev numbers

Jev outputs are **judgments over the state we wrote**, not measurements of the
world. Reading rules:

- **Probability ≠ confidence.** A Noul of 0.79 means "lean yes"; its
  usefulness depends on state quality, not on the decimal.
- **Confidence is a second axis** (distribution shape). Gate actions on it —
  difficulty changes only at ≥0.6 (`jev-integration.md`); low-confidence
  answers read as "no signal," not "no."
- **The 001/007 lesson is the control case:** P=0.95 at confidence 0.94 for
  the *wrong* audience, because the state was wrong. A confident answer to a
  badly-stated question is our most expensive failure mode — that's why
  `question-audit.md` exists.
- Policy stays in code. Jev ranks, code decides, fallbacks always work offline.

## 4. What may be said, and where

| | Internal (docs, prioritization) | External (copy, store, in-app) |

## 5. The rule

Numbers in §1 are for **us** — they justify why processing speed got a pillar
score, why numeracy didn't, why the reminiscence game leads the matrix. None
of them may reach a user as a promise. The product's honest claim is narrow
and sufficient: *pleasant, dignified mental exercise that respects the player.*
